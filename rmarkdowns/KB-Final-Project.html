What SDG 2 Really Shows Us about Hunger

Setup

#To Label and Clean Data

sdr_data <- read_csv(here("data/SDR-2023-Data.csv"))

sdr_data <- sdr_data %>% 
  clean_names()

#Assign Value for World Data

world <- ne_countries(scale = "medium", returnclass = "sf") %>%
  left_join(
    sdr_data %>% 
      select(iso3_code = country_code_iso3, normalized_score_sdg2_undernsh),
    by = c("iso_a3" = "iso3_code")
  )

#Mapping time

#The WORLD gets Mapped 
ggplot(world) +
  geom_sf(aes(fill = normalized_score_sdg2_undernsh), color = "white", size = 0.1) +
  scale_fill_viridis_c(
    option = "magma",
    direction = -1,
    name = "Hunger Score",
    na.value = "grey"
  ) +
  labs(
    title = "Progress Toward Zero Hunger (SDG 2)",
    subtitle = "Higher scores show better hunger outcomes towards 0 hunger goal",
  ) +
  theme_void() +
  theme(
    plot.title = element_text(face = "bold", hjust = 0.5),
    plot.subtitle = element_text(hjust = 0.5),
    legend.position = "bottom"
  )

Breakdown

This map gives us an overview of every country’s progress towards achieving SDG (Sustainable Development Goal) 2: Zero Hunger. I have each country colored based on its score, with darker colors meaning achieved or in the process of achieving, and light colors not as much. This way, we can see which areas of the world undernourishment still is a major issue.

Looking at the map, it’s clear that Africa is showing the most difficulties with this issue, with many countries in the region having the lowest scores and standing out in lighter shades. As for the rest of the world, though there are other areas. Places such as Europe and North America, generally show much higher scores and darker colors, which gives us the indication that hunger is much less of a problem for those countries. This shows us how uneven progress toward zero hunger is global. By mapping the scores like this, we can see the regions where more attention and resources are needed.

Overall, this map here makes it easier to see not just which countries are doing well, but also where hunger is still a serious challenge.

  labs(
    title = "Double Challenge of Malnutrition",

    y = "Undernourishment Score (Lower = Worse)",
    color = "Region" 
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
## `geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x'

Breakdown

This scatterplot lets us check if countries with childhood obesity also struggle with undernourishment. Each dot is a country, and I’ve made them different colors based on which part of the world they’re in, just to make it easier to spot patterns. The slanted line running through the plot is there to show if a general trend exists-even if it’s not a very strong one in this case. At the very top, there’s a row of dots: these mark countries that got a perfect score for undernourishment, which basically means they’ve hit the SDG 2 goal for zero hunger. Most of those top scorers are in Europe or North America.

When I look at the scatter, I don’t see much of a link between obesity and undernourishment. Some places have high obesity but aren’t struggling with hunger, and others have low scores for both-or even both issues showing up at once. Before I made this graph, I filtered out any countries that didn’t have data for either variable, just so we’re only looking at the complete and reliable numbers. This does mean a handful of countries are left off the chart.

All in all, this scatterplot shows that having lots of obesity doesn’t always mean hunger is gone-and vice versa. Different countries and regions are dealing with their own mix of nutrition problems, and there’s no one-size-fits-all story here.

    y = "Hunger Score (Higher = Less Hunger)",
    color = "Region"
  ) +
  theme_minimal()
## `geom_smooth()` using formula = 'y ~ x'

Breakdown

After looking at the big map and getting a sense of where hunger is still a problem (especially in places like Africa), and then seeing that hunger and obesity don’t always go hand-in-hand, I wanted to dig deeper into why hunger hangs on in some countries. One thing that seemed obvious to check is whether countries that struggle with hunger just aren’t able to grow enough food. So, for this plot, I compared each country’s crop yield score with its hunger score, and again, I’ve color-coded by region to make it easier to spot patterns.

Just like before, I filtered out any countries that were missing data for either of these scores, so what you see here is based on reliable info only. The line through the plot shows if there’s any trend, but I noticed this relationship isn’t super strong. Some places that have good crop yields still show a lot of hunger, while others with not as great yields appear here to be doing okay on hunger. That tells me there’s more to the story than just how much food is being grown; things like food distribution, affordability, and maybe even politics or climate could be making a big difference.

Basically, growing more food doesn’t guarantee the end of hunger, and you can see that in these dots and the spread on this chart. This plot kind of ties everything together so far: hunger is a complex issue, and it’s about way more than just farming harder.
